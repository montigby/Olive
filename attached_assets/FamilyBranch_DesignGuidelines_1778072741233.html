01
Know Your User
Before any design decision, understand who you are designing for. Not a persona. A real person.

"She is not afraid of her phone. She is afraid of breaking something, looking stupid, or losing the progress she made."

Design Principle · FamilyBranch
The Primary User: Mom (40–65)
She runs a group chat, uses Facebook to see photos of grandkids, and takes screenshots of things she doesn't want to forget. She is the family organizer. She is the one who will set up FamilyBranch, invite everyone, and keep it going. She needs to feel competent. She needs to feel like she is doing something good for her family. She does not need to feel like she is operating software.
The Secondary User: Grandma (60–80)
She was invited by her daughter. She does not entirely understand what she signed up for. She will use the app on a phone with large text settings enabled. She will tap things slowly and deliberately. She needs the interface to be patient and forgiving. She should never encounter a screen that makes her feel like she made a mistake. Every action she takes should feel rewarded.
The Tertiary User: Family Members (18–50)
They received an invite link. They are mildly skeptical. They will claim their profile because mom or grandma asked them to. The experience must be fast, frictionless, and not embarrassing to use. They should not feel like they joined a genealogy website.
Design Reminder
The senior user is not the edge case. She is the core user. Design for her first. The younger user will adapt. The older user will abandon.
02
Design Principles
Six principles that govern every decision. When in doubt, return to these.
  1. Warmth over efficiency. The product is about family. It should feel like a kitchen table conversation, not a dashboard. Every screen should feel welcoming. Cold, clinical UI belongs in enterprise software. Not here.
  2. One thing at a time. Never ask the user to do two things at once. One question per screen during onboarding. One primary action per view. Cognitive load is the enemy. Especially for seniors.
  3. Generous targets. Every tappable element is large enough to hit without squinting or precision. Minimum 48px tap target on every interactive element. Buttons span full width wherever possible.
  4. Error forgiveness. Nothing is permanent without explicit confirmation. Mistakes are easy to undo. The user should never feel afraid to tap something. Fear kills engagement.
  5. Progress is visible. The user always knows where she is, how far she has come, and what comes next. Progress indicators, completion states, and confirmation moments make her feel successful.
  6. Language before interface. When in doubt, say it plainly. A well-worded prompt beats a clever UI pattern every time. Write like a warm, patient friend. Not like an app.

Hard Rule
Never use pure red (#FF0000 or similar) as an error color. It is alarming to seniors and reads as failure. Use Dusty Rose (#C4826A) instead. The problem is still communicated. The emotional register is warmer and less catastrophic.

Hard Rule · Minimum Sizes
Body text minimum: 15px. Caption text minimum: 12px. Button text minimum: 16px. No exceptions. Many of our users have their system font size set to Large or Extra Large. The app must not fight that setting. Use relative units (rem) wherever possible so system font size preferences are respected.
Why Cormorant Garamond
Serif display fonts read as trustworthy, personal, and warm to women in our target demographic. They feel like a letter, not a notification. Cormorant specifically has a graceful, feminine quality without being frilly or dated. It signals that this is not another tech product. It is something made with care.

Hard Rule · Button Labels
Button labels must be plain, action-oriented, and specific. Never use "Submit", "OK", "Next", or "Continue" alone. Always say what happens: "Create My Family Tree", "Send Invite to Spencer", "Save My Profile". The user should know exactly what will happen before she taps.
Form Inputs
Inputs are large, clearly labeled, and forgiving. The focused state is obvious. Errors never feel like failure.

07
Voice & Language
The words matter as much as the visual design. For this audience, language is design.
Tone of Voice
Write like a warm, patient daughter who works in tech. Knowledgeable but never condescending. Encouraging without being saccharine. Clear without being blunt. The product should feel like it is on the user's side.

Key Copy Rules
ContextRuleExample
Screen titles Short. Conversational. No verbs ending in -ing. "Your Family" not "Managing Your Family Members"
Error messages Say what happened and what to do. Never blame the user. "This link has expired. Ask Miranda to send you a new one."
Empty states Encouraging, not cold. Tell the user what to do next. "No members yet. Tap + Add to invite your first family member."
Success messages Celebratory but brief. Acknowledge the action. "Profile saved!" or "You're in the family."
Placeholder text Show format only. Never put instructions in placeholders. "(801) 555-0100" not "Enter your phone number here"
Privacy language Be specific and simple. Name who can and cannot see the information. "Only your family can see this. We never share it." not "Your data is private."
Tech terms to avoid Never use: sync, onboard, node, user, admin, configure, authenticate. Instead: update, join, family member, person in charge, set up, sign in.

Accessibility
Accessibility for this product is not a compliance checkbox. It is the product. Our senior users depend on it.
RequirementStandardFamilyBranch Spec
Text contrast WCAG AA 4.5:1 Target 7:1 (AAA) for all body text Required
Minimum font size WCAG: no minimum 15px body · 12px caption · Hard floor Required
Tap target size Apple HIG: 44px · WCAG: 44px 48px minimum on all interactive elements Required
System font scaling iOS Dynamic Type All text in rem units. Must not break layout at Large and Extra Large font settings. Required
Color not sole indicator WCAG 1.4.1 Claimed vs. unclaimed is always differentiated by label text AND color, never color alone. Required
Error identification WCAG 3.3.1 Errors identified in text, not color only. Error message appears inline under the affected field. Required
VoiceOver / TalkBack iOS / Android screen readers All interactive elements have descriptive accessibility labels. Images have alt text. Required
Focus indicators WCAG 2.4.7 Visible focus ring on all focusable elements. 2px solid Sage on all inputs. Required
Reduce motion prefers-reduced-motion Respect system preference. Any animations are disabled when reduced motion is enabled. Required
Senior-Specific Considerations
Many women in our 60–80 demographic have mild cataracts, reduced contrast sensitivity, or early macular degeneration. Design for this. High contrast text, generous white space, and large clear targets are not accommodations. They are good design decisions that benefit every user. Test the app with the iOS accessibility large text setting enabled before every release.

10
Anti-Patterns
Things that commonly appear in consumer apps and must never appear in FamilyBranch. This list is a standing veto for any feature proposal.
Anti-PatternWhy It Fails Our AudienceWhat To Do Instead
Dark mode as default Seniors find dark interfaces harder to read. Text-on-dark-bg reduces legibility for reduced contrast sensitivity. Light mode only at MVP. If dark mode is added later, it must be opt-in and never the default.
Swipe-only navigation Swipe gestures are not discoverable and require motor precision. Seniors often trigger them accidentally or miss them entirely. All primary actions have visible tap targets. Swipe can be an enhancement, never the only method.
Icon-only bottom nav Icons without labels require memorization. The senior user does not want to memorize what a leaf icon means. Every bottom nav item has an icon AND a text label. Always.
Auto-dismissing toasts Success and error messages that disappear after 2 seconds are missed by slower readers. Toasts persist for at least 4 seconds. Critical errors stay until dismissed manually.
Infinite scroll Disorienting. The user loses her place. No clear sense of progress or completion. Paginated lists or clearly bounded scrollable sections with visible end states.
Hamburger menus Not discoverable. Seniors often do not know what the three-line icon means or that it contains navigation. All primary navigation in the bottom tab bar. Settings accessible from a clearly labeled icon in the app nav.
Micro-copy that assumes tech literacy "Sync your contacts", "Link your account", "Configure your settings" assumes vocabulary our user does not share. Plain language always. "Update your info", "Connect to your family", "Change your preferences".
Skipping confirmation on destructive actions Seniors are more likely to tap something accidentally. Permanent actions without confirmation create anxiety about using the app at all. Any action that deletes data or removes a member requires a plain-language confirmation dialog with cancel as the default.
Social pressure patterns "3 people are viewing this profile right now" style patterns create anxiety, not engagement. FamilyBranch is private and calm. No notification counts, urgency language, or social proof pressure.
Gamification Streaks, badges, and points feel juvenile and out of place in a product about family relationships. Progress is communicated through meaningful completion states, not abstract scores.
The Grandma Test
Before shipping any screen, ask: could my grandmother use this without help? If the honest answer is no, the screen is not ready. This is not a hypothetical. Test with real women in the 60–75 age range before launch. What you discover will be humbling and essential.
